Part 1

The problem

A parametrised policy, one number to maximise, and a running example we can draw.

RecapThe objective, written as a function of the policy

Start state $S_0 \sim \rho$ (in the maze: $\rho = \delta_{s_1}$). The value of a policy is one number:

$$J(\pi) \;=\; \E_\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R_{t+1}\Big] \;=\; \sum_s \rho(s)\, \Vpi(s)$$
  • Control = $\max_\pi J(\pi)$.
  • Lectures 2 to 5: get there through value functions: compute or learn $\Qstar$, then act greedily.
  • Today: parametrise the policy, $\pi_\theta$, and maximise $J(\theta) := J(\pi_\theta)$ over $\theta \in \R^p$.

Parametrised policies

tabular softmax$\propto \exp(\theta_{s,a})$, $\;\theta \in \R^{|\S|\times|\A|}$

neural softmax$\propto \exp(f_\theta(s)_a)$: a network with $|\A|$ outputs

Requirement: $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ is differentiable in $\theta$ (and positive wherever we need to explore).

Emphasise: for a neural softmax policy the architecture can be identical to DQN; the difference is only what the outputs mean and how they are trained.

Running exampleA policy with two parameters

Two hand-made features, one per behaviour:

  • $\phi_{\text{big}}(s,a) = 1$ if $a$ is the first move of the shortest route from $s$ to the big exit, else $0$
  • $\phi_{\text{small}}(s,a) = 1$ if $a$ is the first move of the shortest route to the small exit, else $0$

A linear softmax policy on these features:

$$\pi_\theta(a|s) \;\propto\; \exp\big(\theta_{\text{big}}\,\phi_{\text{big}}(s,a) + \theta_{\text{small}}\,\phi_{\text{small}}(s,a)\big)$$

Two numbers: $\theta = (\theta_{\text{big}}, \theta_{\text{small}}) \in \R^2$, the weight given to each behaviour.

  • $\theta = (0,0)$: all logits $0$, the uniform policy.
  • $\theta = (5, 0)$: in $s_1$, $\pi(\rightarrow|s_1) = e^5/(e^5+3) \approx 0.98$: follows the big route. Negative weights mean "avoid".
  • The two features agree only in $s_3$, $s_4$, $s_{12}$.
Why this parametrisation: (1) two parameters, so we can draw J(θ) and every update in the plane; (2) it is a genuine linear softmax policy, the score is φ(s,a) − E_π[φ(s,·)]; (3) it contains near-optimal policies (θ_big → ∞) and a local optimum (θ_small → ∞), so it shows the non-concavity of J with only two numbers; (4) the same family is used in every demo today: CEM, the REINFORCE cloud, baselines, REINFORCE runs. Shortest routes are computed by breadth-first search in the maze; ties are broken in the order ↑ → ↓ ←, which is why s1 points up (not right) for the small exit.

Running exampleThe landscape $J(\theta)$

Part 2

Black-box policy search

Before computing any gradient: try parameters, keep the good ones.

Policy search without gradients

We cannot write $J(\theta)$ down, but we can evaluate it: run $\pi_\theta$ for $M$ trajectories and average their returns.

$$\hat J(\theta) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M R(\tau_i), \qquad \tau_i \sim p_\theta, \qquad \E\big[\hat J(\theta)\big] = J(\theta)$$
  • Treat $\theta \mapsto \hat J(\theta)$ as a noisy black box: zero-order optimisation.
  • Many options: hill climbing, finite differences, Nelder–Mead, evolution strategies…
  • $\pi_\theta$ does not even need to be differentiable.
Discuss with your neighbour A trajectory contains every state, action and reward. What does $\hat J(\theta)$ keep of it?
Finite differences in one line if asked: ∂J/∂θ_k ≈ (Ĵ(θ + ε u_k) − Ĵ(θ))/ε needs p + 1 noisy evaluations per gradient. Kohl & Stone (2004) tuned the 12 gait parameters of an Aibo robot dog this way. Answer to the question: only one number per trajectory, R(τ).

The cross-entropy method (CEM)

CEM Initialise a search distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2)$ over $\theta \in \R^p$
  1. Sample a population $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2)$
  2. Score each one: $\hat J(\theta_n)$ from $M$ trajectories
  3. Keep the $k$ best: the elites
  4. Refit $\mu, \sigma$: mean and standard deviation of the elites, per coordinate (plus a small noise floor)
  5. Go to 1
  • The randomness is in the parameters, not in the actions: each candidate $\theta_n$ is a whole policy.
  • Step 4 is maximum likelihood on the elites: it minimises the cross-entropy between the elite sample and the search distribution.
  • Cost: $N \times M$ trajectories per iteration.
  • Classic results: Tetris (Szita & Lőrincz, 2006). Refined versions: CMA-ES.
Rubinstein (1999) for the method. The noise floor keeps σ from collapsing too early (Szita & Lőrincz call it "noisy CEM").

MazeCEM on the running example

Start at μ = (0, 0) (the uniform policy), σ = 2. Alternate "sample population" and "refit to elites" twice, slowly; then "iterate ×5". Over 40 seeds, this setting reaches the big exit every time after 15 iterations, using 1500 trajectories (20 candidates × 5 trajectories × 15). Point out that J is bimodal: candidates near the small-exit plateau score ≈ 0.9 and are outscored. Here, in 2D, CEM works very well. The question is how it scales with p.

What CEM ignores

  • One number per trajectory: $R(\tau)$. Which actions were good, and when? Lost.
  • The population must explore every direction of $\R^p$. Fine for $p = 2$ or $p = 12$ (a robot gait), hard for a network with $10^6$ weights.
  • But: simple, parallel, robust to noise, works for non-differentiable policies. Evolution strategies remain a strong baseline (Salimans et al., 2017).
Discuss with your neighbour Other (better) ideas?
Collect ideas. Two we follow next: perturb one coordinate at a time (finite differences), or use the actions we took (log-derivative trick). CEM increases the likelihood of the parameters that worked; policy gradient will increase the likelihood of the actions that worked.
Part 3

The gradient, from trajectories

Use what CEM throws away: the actions we took.

So, what is $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$?

$$J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim p_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\big] = \sum_\tau \cpol{p_\theta(\tau)}\, R(\tau), \qquad R(\tau) = \sum_{t} \gamma^t R_{t+1}$$
  • The return $R(\tau)$ of a given trajectory does not depend on $\theta$. The parameters only change which trajectories we see, through $p_\theta(\tau)$.
  • $p_\theta(\tau) = \crew{\rho(S_0)} \prod_t \cpol{\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)}\, \crew{P(S_{t+1}|S_t,A_t)}$: a part we know and can differentiate, and the dynamics, which we don't know.
Discuss with your neighbour How could we estimate $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ without knowing $\crew{P}$?
Two ideas follow: perturb θ and compare returns (finite differences), or differentiate p_θ(τ) itself (log-derivative trick).

Idea 1: finite differences

Finite-difference gradient For each coordinate $k = 1, \dots, p$ of $\theta$, perturb it by a small $\varepsilon$ and compare the returns: $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_k}(\theta) \;\approx\; \frac{\hat J(\theta + \varepsilon\, e_k) - \hat J(\theta)}{\varepsilon},$$ where $e_k$ is the $k$-th unit vector and $\hat J$ is the average return of $M$ trajectories.
  • Cost: $p + 1$ policies to evaluate, $M$ trajectories each: $(p+1)M$ trajectories for one gradient step.
  • Noise: the errors of $\hat J$ are divided by $\varepsilon$. Small $\varepsilon$: huge noise. Large $\varepsilon$: not a gradient any more.
  • Works for any policy, differentiable or not.

Example: Kohl & Stone (2004) tuned the 12 gait parameters of an Aibo robot dog this way, timing each gait on the field.

Idea 2 Can we get the whole gradient from the trajectories of $\pi_\theta$ alone, without perturbing $\theta$?
Same flavour as CEM: θ is evaluated as a black box, one number per trajectory. In the running example p = 2, so 3 policies per step; for a network, millions.

Idea 2: write the gradient as an expectation

Simplest case first: a one-step MDP. In state $s$, pick $a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)$, receive $r(s,a)$, the episode ends.

Tool: log-derivative trick $\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta = \pi_\theta\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$, since $\nabla \log \pi = \nabla \pi / \pi$.
$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$: the score function.
$$J(\theta) = \E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[r(s,a)\big] = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s)\, r(s,a)$$
the objective, written as a sum over actions
$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_a \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s)}\; r(s,a)$$
$r$ does not depend on $\theta$. Problem: this needs $r(s,a)$ for every action, and $\nabla\pi$ is not a probability.
$$\textcolor{#8a9086}{\nabla_\theta J(\theta)} = \sum_a \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\pi_\theta(a|s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)}\; r(s,a)$$
log-derivative trick (left)
$$\textcolor{#8a9086}{\nabla_\theta J(\theta)} = \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\; r(s,a)\, \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\big]}$$
a sum weighted by $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ is an expectation under $\pi_\theta$
The gradient is an expectation Sample $a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)$, observe $r$: $\;\hat g = r\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$, from the action we took only.
Same structure as Georg's slide. Two separate messages: the log-derivative trick is only a tool (left box); the point is the last line: the gradient is an expectation under our own policy, so we can estimate it by sampling. Line 2 is the problem: a sum over all actions with weights ∇π that are not probabilities. Lines 3–4 turn it into an average over actions drawn from π_θ. Intuition: ĝ pushes up the log-probability of the action we took, in proportion to its reward. Next slide: the same steps for whole trajectories.

Whole trajectories: the dynamics cancel

Recall $J(\theta) = \textstyle\sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau)$
probability of $\tau$ × its return

Same steps, with a whole trajectory $\tau$ instead of one action, and $p_\theta(\tau)$ instead of $\pi_\theta(a|s)$:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_\tau \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) = \E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[R(\tau)\, \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)}\big]$$
same steps as for one action: log-derivative trick, then a weighted sum is an expectation
$$\log p_\theta(\tau) = \log \crew{\rho(S_0)} + \sum_t \log \cpol{\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)} + \sum_t \log \crew{P(S_{t+1}|S_t,A_t)}$$
$p_\theta(\tau)$ is a product of probabilities, so its log is a sum
$$\htmlClass{pg-hl}{\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)} = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \cpol{\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)}$$
$\crew{\rho}$ and $\crew{P}$ do not depend on $\theta$: their gradients are $0$
Policy gradient (Williams, 1992) $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim p_\theta}\Big[\, R(\tau) \sum_{t \ge 0} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \Big]$$ No $\crew{P}$: only the scores of the actions we took, and the return. "Reinforce" what worked.
Σ_τ is over all possible trajectories, not over sampled ones and not over time steps: it is the trajectory analogue of Σ_a on the previous slide. Sampled trajectories τ_1, …, τ_N only appear when we estimate the expectation (REINFORCE). The returns in the drawing are real values from the maze with γ = 0.9: big exit after 6 steps 2.95, after 8 steps 2.39, after 10 steps 1.94; small exit after 2 steps 0.90, after 4 steps 0.73. Technicalities (exchanging ∇ and the infinite sum) are fine for finite MDPs, bounded rewards and smooth policies. With one step this is exactly the previous slide.

Policy$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$

Let's practice Derive the linear-softmax line from $\log \pi_\theta(a|s) = \theta^\top\phi(s,a) - \log \sum_b e^{\theta^\top \phi(s,b)}$. Check that the score has mean zero: $\E_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\big] = 0$.
Solution click to hide $$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) &= \phi(s,a) - \frac{\nabla_\theta \sum_b e^{\theta^\top\phi(s,b)}}{\sum_{b'} e^{\theta^\top\phi(s,b')}} = \phi(s,a) - \sum_b \underbrace{\frac{e^{\theta^\top\phi(s,b)}}{\sum_{b'} e^{\theta^\top\phi(s,b')}}}_{=\,\pi_\theta(b|s)} \phi(s,b) \\ &= \phi(s,a) - \E_{b\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[\phi(s,b)\big] \end{aligned}$$ Mean zero: $\;\E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\phi(s,a)\big] - \E_{b\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\phi(s,b)\big] = 0$. In general: $\sum_a \pi_\theta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta = \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$.
Mean zero: Σ_a π ∇log π = Σ_a ∇π = ∇ 1 = 0. Keep this in mind: it is the reason baselines work.
Part 4

REINFORCE

Monte Carlo policy gradient.

From an expectation to a sample

The gradient is an expectation over trajectories of $\pi_\theta$, so we can estimate it with one specific trajectory. Run $\pi_\theta$ once and record:

$$\tilde\tau = \big(S_0, A_0, R_1,\; S_1, A_1, R_2,\; \dots,\; S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T\big) \sim p_\theta$$
$$\hat g(\tilde\tau) = R(\tilde\tau) \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t), \qquad \E_{\tilde\tau\sim p_\theta}\big[\hat g(\tilde\tau)\big] = \nabla_\theta J(\theta)$$

$S_t, A_t$: the states and actions of $\tilde\tau$. $\;R(\tilde\tau) = \sum_t \gamma^t R_{t+1}$: its return, one number.

  • No model, no value function, no Bellman equation.
  • Averaging over $N$ trajectories divides the variance by $N$, and costs $N$ times more interaction.
Compare with CEM CEM reinforced the parameters that worked; $\hat g$ reinforces the actions that worked.

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992)

REINFORCE, first version
  1. Initialise $\theta$ (e.g. uniform policy)
  2. Repeat:
    1. Run $\pi_\theta$: $\;S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T$
    2. $R(\tau) = \sum_{t} \gamma^t R_{t+1}$
    3. $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\, R(\tau) \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)$
  • Model-free, on-policy: the trajectory must come from the current $\pi_\theta$. Old trajectories cannot be reused as is.
  • In code: loss = -R * sum(logp), then let autodiff do the rest.
  • Stochastic gradient ascent: decreasing $\alpha$, or mini-batches of trajectories.
  • Part 5: how noisy is this in practice, and a first fix: causality.

Let's practice: one REINFORCE update by hand

Tabular softmax, $\theta = 0$ (uniform), $\gamma = 0.9$, $\alpha = 1$. One trajectory:

$$s_1 \xrightarrow{\;\rightarrow\;} s_2 \xrightarrow{\;\uparrow\;} \text{small exit } (+1)$$

  1. Compute $R(\tau)$.
  2. Write the score $\nabla_{\theta_{s,\cdot}} \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ at $\theta = 0$.
  3. Which entries of $\theta$ change, and by how much?
  4. New value of $\pi_\theta(\rightarrow|s_1)$?
Solution $R(\tau) = 0 + \gamma \cdot 1 = 0.9$. Score $= e_a - \tfrac14\mathbf{1}$. Both steps get weight $R(\tau) = 0.9$, so $\theta_{s_1,\rightarrow} = \theta_{s_2,\uparrow} = 0.675$, and the 3 other entries of rows $s_1$, $s_2$ are $-0.225$. Then $\pi(\rightarrow|s_1) = \frac{e^{0.675}}{e^{0.675} + 3e^{-0.225}} \approx 0.45$.
Discussion: the trajectory went to the small exit, yet we increased π(→|s1), which is also the first move of the big-exit route. With r ≥ 0, every sampled action gets pushed up; only the relative sizes of the pushes carry information. This motivates baselines.

Variance and causality

Unbiased is not enough.

Why is $\hat g$ so noisy?

  • One trajectory = one sample of a random vector.
  • $R(\tau)$ carries the randomness of every action and transition of the trajectory.
  • $\hat g$ sums $T$ score terms: the noise grows with the horizon.
  • All returns are $\ge 0$ here: every action taken is reinforced, even in a bad trajectory. The signal is only in the differences between pushes.
One free lunch The score has mean zero in every state: $$\E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\big] = \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0.$$ So any term of $\hat g$ that multiplies a score by something the action cannot influence has mean zero: we can drop it without bias.

A toolAveraging in two stages

A game: flip a coin. Heads: roll a die and win the number shown. Tails: win 1 €. What do you win on average?

Directly, over all 7 outcomes:

$$\E[W] = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac16\,(1+\dots+6) + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = 2.25\text{ €}$$

In two stages: average what happens after the coin, for each side; then average over the coin:

$$\E[W] = \tfrac12\cdot \htmlClass{pg-hl}{3.5} + \tfrac12 \cdot \htmlClass{pg-hl}{1} = 2.25\text{ €}$$
Tower rule $$\E[X] = \E\big[\, \E[X \mid Y] \,\big]$$ $\E[X \mid Y]$: the average of $X$ once $Y$ is known. Here $Y$ = the coin and $\E[W \mid \text{heads}] = 3.5$.
Let them compute the first line themselves (2.25). The point of the two-stage computation: we can first fix what happened early (the coin), average over what happens later, and only then average over the early part. This is the only probability tool needed for causality. Formally E[X | Y] is a random variable, a function of Y; no need to say more.

A toolSomething fixed before, times a fair coin

First the weather $Y$ is decided, and it sets your stake $f(Y)$. Then a fair coin $Z = \pm 1$ decides if you win or lose it. You win $f(Y)\cdot Z$.

$Z = +1$ (½)$Z = -1$ (½)average
sunny (¾): stake 10 €$+10$$-10$$0$

In REINFORCE

The score plays the fair coin: given the state, Σ_a π(a|s) ∇log π(a|s) = 0 (slide "Why is ĝ so noisy?", and the score exercise). Unlike a real coin it is a vector and not ±1, but its average given S_t is 0, which is all we use.

Causality (1/2): an action cannot change the past

$\mathcal{H}_t = (S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_t)$: the history before $A_t$. For $k \lt t$, $R_{k+1}$ is part of $\mathcal{H}_t$ (a "stake"), so by the tower rule:

$$\E\big[R_{k+1}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big] = \E\Big[R_{k+1}\, \underbrace{\E\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \,\big|\, \mathcal{H}_t\big]}_{=\,0 \text{ (score has mean zero)}}\Big] = 0 \qquad \text{for } k \lt t \qquad (\star)$$
$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \E\Big[\sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\; \sum_{k} \gamma^k R_{k+1}\Big] = \sum_t \sum_k \gamma^k\, \E\big[R_{k+1}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big]$$
the trajectory form, expanded: one term per pair (score at time $t$, reward at time $k+1$)
$$\textcolor{#8a9086}{\nabla_\theta J(\theta)} = \sum_t \Big( \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\sum_{k \lt t} \gamma^k\, \E\big[R_{k+1}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big]} + \sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^k\, \E\big[R_{k+1}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big] \Big)$$
split each sum: rewards before $A_t$ ($k \lt t$), and rewards from $A_t$ on ($k \ge t$). The highlighted terms are $0$ by $(\star)$
$$\textcolor{#8a9086}{\nabla_\theta J(\theta)} = \E\Big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\, \htmlClass{pg-hl}{\sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^k R_{k+1}}\Big] = \E\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t\, G_t\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\Big]$$
put the sum back inside the expectation, and factor $\gamma^t$: $\;\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^k R_{k+1} = \gamma^t \sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^{k-t} R_{k+1} = \gamma^t G_t$, the reward-to-go

REINFORCE as it is usually written: each score weighted only by the rewards after its action.

Most implementations also drop the γ^t in front of G_t. That is no longer the gradient of J (Nota & Thomas, 2020), but it often works well in practice.

Row $t$ keeps the rewards from time $t$ on: $\psi_t\, \gamma^t G_t$.

REINFORCE with reward-to-go Repeat:
1. Run $\pi_\theta$: $\;S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T$
2. For $t = T-1, \dots, 0$: $\;G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1}$ ($G_T = 0$)
3. $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t} \gamma^t\, \htmlClass{pg-hl}{G_t}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)$
  • The dropped terms have mean $0$ but not variance $0$: they are pure noise.
  • The longer the trajectory and the more rewards along the way, the more terms we drop (about half of $T^2$).
  • In our two-exit maze all the reward comes at the end: $\gamma^t G_t = R(\tau)$ for every $t$, so causality gains nothing here. To see it work, the next demos charge a cost of 0.2 per step: rewards along the way.
Make the table appear row by row if you like. The take-away: causality is free, never hurts, and its benefit depends on where the rewards are.

θ = (0.5, −0.5), as in the earlier demos. "sample 20" with "full return", then with "reward-to-go": the cloud shrinks, the centre (ring) stays. "compare estimators": E‖ĝ − ∇J‖² over 2000 trajectories ≈ 11.4 for the full return vs ≈ 3.0 with reward-to-go (‖∇J‖² ≈ 0.57). About four times less variance, for free. (At θ = 0 it is 9.0 vs 1.9, but there the step cost makes the exact gradient point towards the small exit.) Why: long trajectories that wander accumulate many −0.2 costs; with the full return these early costs multiply every later score, with reward-to-go they don't.

Summary

  • Policy search: $\max_\theta J(\theta)$. Already with two parameters, $J$ has two basins.
  • Black-box ideas (CEM, finite differences): simple, but one number per trajectory, and they scale badly with $\dim\theta$.
  • Log-derivative trick: the dynamics cancel, $\;\nabla J = \E\big[R(\tau)\sum_t \nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big]$.
  • REINFORCE: estimate it with sampled trajectories. Unbiased, on-policy, very noisy: on a non-concave $J$, noise changes where we end up.
  • Causality: an action cannot change the past. Weight each score by the reward-to-go $\gamma^t G_t$: no bias, less variance.

Baselines (1/2): compare with a reference return

Pick a number $b$, given and fixed before we see the trajectory. It must not depend on the actions of $\tau$.

$$\E\Big[\, b \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \Big] \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= b \sum_t \E\Big[\underbrace{\E\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \,\big|\, S_t\big]}_{=\,0}\Big]} \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= 0}$$
Unbiased for any such $b$ $$\hat g_b = \big(R(\tau) - \cfut{b}\big) \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t), \qquad \E\big[\hat g_b\big] = \nabla_\theta J(\theta).$$

$b$ is our reference: what return do we usually get? Trajectories that do better than $b$ push their actions up, the others push them down.

Insist: for now b is a number we choose, fixed during the update. Not learned, not state-dependent. A baseline b(s) that depends on the state is possible too, but it needs the step-by-step view of the gradient: next week.

Baselines (2/2): every $b$ is unbiased, not every $b$ helps

BaselineComment
$b = 0$plain REINFORCE
$b = 3$arbitrary constant: still unbiased, but it can make the variance worse
$b = $ running averageaverage of the returns seen so far: practical, needs no model
$b = J(\theta)$the exact average return $V^{\pi_\theta}(s_1)$: an oracle, computed from the model
Intuition Without a baseline, every action is pushed up (all returns are $\ge 0$). With a good reference, actions of trajectories that did worse than usual are pushed down: the sign carries information.
In the demos Running average: $b$ = first return, then $b \leftarrow b + 0.3\,(R(\tau) - b)$ after each trajectory. The oracle $J(\theta)$ is only there for comparison.
Variance intuition (one step): it is driven by E[(R − b)² ‖∇log π‖²], so b should be close to the returns we typically see. A constant far from the returns (b = 3; returns are in [0, 2.95]) inflates every term. The variance-minimising constant is in the appendix.

Self-studyWhich baseline minimises the variance?

One-step case: $g = (G - b)\, \nabla \log \pi_\theta(A|S)$ and a constant $b$ (per coordinate $i$). Since $\E[g]$ does not depend on $b$, minimise the second moment:

$$\frac{d}{db}\E\big[(G - b)^2 (\partial_i \log\pi_\theta)^2\big] = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_i^\star = \frac{\E\big[G\, (\partial_i \log\pi_\theta)^2\big]}{\E\big[(\partial_i \log\pi_\theta)^2\big]}$$
  • A score-weighted average return: not exactly $V^\pi$, but close when $G$ and the score magnitude are weakly correlated.
  • $V^\pi(s)$ is a natural choice: one number per state, close to $b^\star$ in many problems.
  • See Greensmith, Bartlett & Baxter (2004) for the state-dependent version.
