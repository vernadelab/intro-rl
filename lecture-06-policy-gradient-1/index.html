Part 1

From values to policies

What we have done so far, and what bothers us about it.

RecapThe objective, written as a function of the policy

Start state $S_0 \sim \rho$ (in the maze: $\rho = \delta_{s_1}$). The value of a policy is one number:

$$J(\pi) \;=\; \E_\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R_{t+1}\Big] \;=\; \sum_s \rho(s)\, \Vpi(s)$$
  • Control = $\max_\pi J(\pi)$.
  • Lectures 2 to 5: get there through value functions: compute or learn $\Qstar$, then act greedily.
  • Today: parametrise the policy, $\pi_\theta$, and maximise $J(\theta) := J(\pi_\theta)$ over $\theta \in \R^p$.

RecapValue-based deep RL: learn $Q$, act greedily

Parametrise $Q_w(s,a)$ (a neural network). Minimise the Bellman error on transitions $(S,A,R,S')$:

$$\mathcal{L}(w) = \E\Big[\big(\underbrace{R + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\textcolor{#b5541c}{w}}(S',a')}_{\text{target}} - Q_{\textcolor{#2b5b84}{w}}(S,A)\big)^2\Big]$$

Then act with $\pi(s) = \argmax_a Q_w(s,a)$, plus $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration.

DQN (Mnih et al., 2015)
  1. Act $\varepsilon$-greedily w.r.t. $Q_w$, store transitions in a replay buffer
  2. Sample a mini-batch, regress $Q_w(S,A)$ on $R + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\textcolor{#b5541c}{w^-}}(S',a')$
  3. Every $C$ steps: $w^- \leftarrow w$
The students saw DQN with Chris. Just recall the loss and the target network; the next slide is the point.

The same $Q$ appears twice

$$\big(R + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{\textcolor{#b5541c}{w}}(S',a') - Q_{\textcolor{#2b5b84}{w}}(S,A)\big)^2$$
  • The target moves when we update $w$: we chase our own tail.
  • The true gradient of $\mathcal{L}$ also differentiates the target (and needs two independent samples of $S'$). In practice we ignore it: a semi-gradient, not the gradient of any fixed objective.
  • Fix: freeze a copy $w^-$ → two networks, plus tricks (replay, Double DQN, …).
  • We fit $Q$ everywhere and precisely, but we only use the ordering of actions in each state.
  • $w \mapsto \argmax_a Q_w(s,a)$ is discontinuous: a tiny change of $w$ can flip the policy.
  • $\max_{a'}$ is hard when actions are continuous (a robot joint torque).
Today Can we optimise the policy itself, with an objective that does not depend on itself?
This is the motivation: Q is an intermediate object, and its regression problem is self-referential. J(θ) is a fixed objective: the only thing that moves when θ changes is the data distribution. We will see that the data distribution is exactly the problem, and the PG theorem is exactly the solution.

Policy search: optimise $\pi_\theta$ directly

$$\max_{\theta \in \R^p} \; J(\theta), \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + \alpha \, \nabla_\theta J(\theta_k)$$
Why it is attractive
  • One fixed objective: the expected return, nothing else
  • Smooth: a small step in $\theta$ is a small change of behaviour
  • Stochastic policies come for free (exploration, partial observability, games)
  • Continuous actions: no $\max_a$ to compute
What we will pay
  • $J$ is not concave in $\theta$: local optima, plateaus
  • On-policy: data must come from the current $\pi_\theta$
  • Gradient estimates have high variance (second half of today)

Main obstacle: how do we compute $\nabla_\theta J$? $J$ depends on $\theta$ through the trajectories, which also depend on the unknown $\crew{P}$.

Parametrised policies

Policy$\pi_\theta(a|s)$
tabular softmax$\propto \exp(\theta_{s,a})$, $\;\theta \in \R^{|\S|\times|\A|}$
linear softmax$\propto \exp(\theta^\top \phi(s,a))$
neural softmax$\propto \exp(f_\theta(s)_a)$: a network with $|\A|$ outputs
Gaussian (continuous $a$)$\mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\theta(s), \sigma^2\big)$

Requirement: $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ is differentiable in $\theta$ (and positive wherever we need to explore).

Emphasise: for a neural softmax policy the architecture can be identical to DQN; the difference is only what the outputs mean and how they are trained.
Part 2

Another perspective on RL

Where does a policy spend its time? The occupancy measure.

Unpacking $J(\pi)$

Write $r(s,a) = \E[R_{t+1} \mid S_t = s, A_t = a]$ and swap the sums:

$$\begin{aligned} J(\pi) &= \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t\, \E_\pi\big[r(S_t, A_t)\big] \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t \sum_{s,a} \P_\pi(S_t = s, A_t = a)\, r(s,a)} \\ &\htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \sum_{s,a} \Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t\, \P_\pi(S_t = s, A_t = a)\Big] r(s,a)} \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \frac{1}{1-\gamma} \sum_{s,a} \cpol{d^\pi(s,a)}\, r(s,a)} \end{aligned}$$
Discounted occupancy measure $$d^\pi(s,a) \;=\; (1-\gamma) \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t\, \P_\pi(S_t = s, A_t = a \mid S_0 \sim \rho), \qquad J(\pi) = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\big\langle d^\pi, r \big\rangle$$
KaTeX \class fragments may not animate; if so, the whole derivation shows at once, which is fine. Insist: the reward r is fixed; everything that depends on π is now in d^π.

What is $d^\pi$?

  • A probability distribution on $\S \times \A$: $\;\sum_{s,a} d^\pi(s,a) = (1-\gamma)\sum_t \gamma^t = 1$.
  • It factorises: $\;d^\pi(s,a) = d^\pi(s)\, \pi(a|s)$, with $d^\pi(s) = \sum_a d^\pi(s,a)$.
  • Discounted fraction of time spent in $(s,a)$. Early visits count more.
  • How to sample from it: draw $T \sim \text{Geometric}(1-\gamma)$, i.e. $\P(T = t) = (1-\gamma)\gamma^t$, run $\pi$ for $T$ steps, output $(S_T, A_T)$.
RL in one line $$\max_\pi \; \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\big\langle \cpol{d^\pi}, \crew{r} \big\rangle$$ Choose where to spend your time, knowing $r$ is fixed.

In the maze the exits are absorbing ($P(G|G,a) = 1$, reward $0$). After exiting, the remaining discounted mass sits at the exit.

The geometric-time interpretation is useful later: an unbiased sample of d^π costs one (truncated) episode.

MazeThe occupancy measure of a few policies

Uniform: most mass near the start and the small exit is reached often (d(small) ≈ 0.43). "→ big exit": mass flows along the route. Play with γ: small γ concentrates d on the start state. Then "+100 episodes": the estimated d̂ approaches d. This is the bridge: anything written as an expectation under d^π can be estimated from episodes.

Policies and occupancy measures are the same thing

Theorem (flow constraints) A vector $d \ge 0$ on $\S\times\A$ is the occupancy measure of some policy if and only if, for every $s'$, $$\sum_{a} d(s',a) \;=\; (1-\gamma)\,\rho(s') \;+\; \gamma \sum_{s,a} \crew{P(s'|s,a)}\, d(s,a).$$ The policy is then recovered as $\;\pi_d(a|s) = d(s,a) \,/\, \sum_b d(s,b)$.

Consequence: RL is a linear program $$\max_{d \ge 0} \; \langle d, r \rangle \quad \text{s.t. flow constraints}$$

  • "Mass in = mass out": the discounted flow out of $s'$ equals what starts there plus what flows in.
  • The objective is linear in $d$, but not in $\pi$ or $\theta$.
  • Its dual has one variable per state… they are the values $V(s)$ (next week).
Proof sketch in the appendix. Do not dwell on the LP today: it is the backbone of next week's regularised view (entropy regulariser on d → soft Bellman equations, TRPO/REPS as mirror descent, Neu et al. 2017).

So, what is $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$?

$$J(\theta) = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a} \cpol{d^{\pi_\theta}(s)}\;\cpol{\pi_\theta(a|s)}\; r(s,a)$$

$\theta$ acts on the return in two ways:

  • Directly: which actions we choose in each state, $\pi_\theta(a|s)$. We know this function, we can differentiate it.
  • Indirectly: which states we visit, $d^{\pi_\theta}(s)$. This goes through the dynamics $\crew{P}$, which we don't know.
Discuss with your neighbour Naively, $\nabla_\theta J$ requires $\nabla_\theta d^{\pi_\theta}(s)$: how a change of policy here changes the visit frequencies everywhere. Can we hope to estimate that from episodes?
Let them think. Answer: you would need finite differences over θ, i.e. many episodes per parameter. The PG theorem says: you never need ∇d. That's the miracle.
Part 3

The policy gradient theorem

First, suppose someone gives us $\nabla_\theta J$. Then, compute it.

MazeGradient ascent, with an oracle for $\nabla_\theta J$

Tabular softmax $\pi_\theta(a|s) \propto e^{\theta_{s,a}}$, start from $\theta_0 = 0$ (uniform policy), $\gamma = 0.9$, exact gradient.

Press ×10 a few times. A long plateau near the small-exit value, then a jump to the big exit around k ≈ 30–50. Toggle "show V^π". Ask: why is it so slow at the start? Because d(s) is tiny for states on the big route: the gradient in a state is proportional to how often we visit it (formula in a few slides). Try α = 10: much faster. Gradient ascent works, so the question is only how to get the gradient.

Warm-up: one step (a bandit)

One decision $A \sim \pi_\theta$, reward $r(A)$. Then $J(\theta) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a)\, r(a)$ and

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a)\, r(a) \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \sum_a \pi_\theta(a)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a)}{\pi_\theta(a)}\, r(a)} \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \E_{A \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\, r(A)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A)\,\big]}$$
Log-derivative trick $\nabla \pi = \pi\, \nabla \log \pi$. The gradient of an expectation becomes an expectation. We can estimate it with samples: $$\hat g = r(A)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A), \qquad A \sim \pi_\theta.$$

Score function $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a)$: the direction in $\theta$ that makes $a$ more likely.

$\hat g$ pushes up the probability of the action we took, in proportion to the reward it got: "reinforce" what worked.

Computing the score $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$

Policy$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$
tabular softmax$\dfrac{\partial \log \pi_\theta(a|s)}{\partial \theta_{s',b}} = \mathbb{1}\{s'=s\}\big(\mathbb{1}\{b=a\} - \pi_\theta(b|s)\big)$
linear softmax$\phi(s,a) - \E_{b\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[\phi(s,b)\big]$
Gaussian, fixed $\sigma$$\dfrac{a - \mu_\theta(s)}{\sigma^2}\, \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)$
neural networkbackprop through $\log \pi_\theta(a|s)$: loss = -logp(a|s) * weight
Let's practice Derive the linear-softmax line from $\log \pi_\theta(a|s) = \theta^\top\phi(s,a) - \log \sum_b e^{\theta^\top \phi(s,b)}$. Check that the score has mean zero: $\E_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\big] = 0$.
Mean zero: Σ_a π ∇log π = Σ_a ∇π = ∇ 1 = 0. Keep this in mind: it is the reason baselines work.

Whole episodes: the dynamics cancel

Probability of a trajectory $\tau = (S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, A_1, \dots)$ under $\pi_\theta$:

$$p_\theta(\tau) = \crew{\rho(S_0)} \prod_{t\ge 0} \cpol{\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)}\; \crew{P(S_{t+1}|S_t,A_t)}$$
$$\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log \crew{\rho(S_0)}}_{0} + \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \cpol{\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)} + \sum_t \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log \crew{P(S_{t+1}|S_t,A_t)}}_{0}$$

Same trick on $J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim p_\theta}[R(\tau)]$, with $R(\tau) = \sum_t \gamma^t R_{t+1}$:

Trajectory form $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim p_\theta}\Big[\, R(\tau) \sum_{t \ge 0} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \Big]$$ No $\crew{P}$, no $\nabla d$: only the scores of the actions we took.
Technicalities (exchanging ∇ and the infinite sum/expectation) are fine for finite MDPs, bounded rewards and smooth policies.

The policy gradient theorem

Theorem (Sutton, McAllester, Singh, Mansour, 2000) For any differentiable policy $\pi_\theta$, $$\begin{aligned}\nabla_\theta J(\theta) &= \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\; \E_{(s,a) \sim \cpol{d^{\pi_\theta}}}\Big[\, Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)\; \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \Big] \\ &= \E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t\ge 0} \gamma^t\, Q^{\pi_\theta}(S_t,A_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\Big]\end{aligned}$$

Compare with the naive chain rule

$\nabla J = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a} \nabla\big[d^{\pi_\theta}(s)\pi_\theta(a|s)\big]\, r(s,a)$: as if we could freeze $d^{\pi_\theta}$ and differentiate only $\pi_\theta$, at the price of replacing the immediate reward $r$ by the long-term value $Q^{\pi_\theta}$.

Take-home The gradient is an expectation under the policy's own data. So it can be estimated from sampled episodes, without knowing $\crew{P}$.
Q accounts for all the downstream effects of changing the action in s: that is exactly what ∇d would have captured. Proof on the next slide, full details in the appendix.

ProofFive lines in matrix form

Lecture 2: $\Vpi = r^\pi + \gamma P^\pi \Vpi$ with $r^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a|s) r(s,a)$, $P^\pi(s,s') = \sum_a \pi(a|s) P(s'|s,a)$. Fix a coordinate $\theta_i$, write $\partial = \partial/\partial\theta_i$.

$$\begin{aligned} \partial V &= \partial r^\pi + \gamma\,(\partial P^\pi)\, V + \gamma P^\pi\, \partial V \\ \htmlClass{fragment}{\partial V} &\htmlClass{fragment}{{}= (I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1}\, q_\theta, \qquad q_\theta(s) := \sum_a \partial\pi_\theta(a|s)\Big(r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)V(s')\Big) = \sum_a \partial\pi_\theta(a|s)\, Q(s,a)}\\ \htmlClass{fragment}{\partial J} &\htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \rho^\top \partial V = \rho^\top (I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1} q_\theta} \htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_s d^\pi(s) \sum_a \partial\pi_\theta(a|s)\, Q(s,a)} \\ &\htmlClass{fragment}{{}= \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{s,a} d^\pi(s)\,\pi_\theta(a|s)\; Q(s,a)\; \partial \log \pi_\theta(a|s).} \end{aligned}$$

Key identity: $\rho^\top (I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1} = \sum_t \gamma^t \rho^\top (P^\pi)^t = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,(d^\pi)^\top$. The occupancy measure appears as a weight, never differentiated.

This is the cleanest proof for the class because it reuses the matrix form from Lecture 2. The unrolled proof and the equivalence with the trajectory form are in the appendix.

Back to the maze demo

For the tabular softmax, the score is $\mathbb{1}\{b=a\} - \pi_\theta(b|s)$, and the theorem gives

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_{s,a}} \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\; \cpol{d^{\pi_\theta}(s)}\; \pi_\theta(a|s)\; \big(Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a) - V^{\pi_\theta}(s)\big)$$
  • $Q - V =: A^{\pi}(s,a)$, the advantage: increase actions that are better than average.
  • Weighted by $d^{\pi_\theta}(s)$: states we rarely visit get tiny updates. This explains the plateau: the uniform policy almost never reaches the states on the big-exit route.
  • With the exact gradient, this converges to the optimal policy (Agarwal et al., 2021), but it can be very slow (Mei et al., 2020).
The demo used exactly this formula (with η = d/(1−γ)). Note the plateau is a property of the exact gradient, before any noise.
Part 4

REINFORCE

Monte Carlo policy gradient.

From an expectation to a sample

$Q^{\pi_\theta}(S_t, A_t)$ is unknown. But the return from time $t$ is an unbiased sample of it (Lecture 3, Monte Carlo):

$$G_t = \sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^{k-t} R_{k+1}, \qquad \E\big[G_t \mid S_t, A_t\big] = Q^{\pi_\theta}(S_t,A_t)$$
Monte Carlo policy gradient estimate From one episode $\tau \sim p_\theta$: $$\hat g(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t\, G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t), \qquad \E\big[\hat g(\tau)\big] = \nabla_\theta J(\theta).$$

Unbiased by the tower rule: condition on $(S_t,A_t)$ inside the trajectory form of the theorem.

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992)

REINFORCE
  1. Initialise $\theta$ (e.g. uniform policy)
  2. Repeat:
    1. Run $\pi_\theta$: $\;S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T$
    2. For $t = T-1, \dots, 0$: $\;G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}$
    3. $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t} \gamma^t\, G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)$
  • Model-free, on-policy: the episode must come from the current $\pi_\theta$. Old episodes cannot be reused as is.
  • In code: loss = -sum(gamma**t * G[t] * logp[t]), then let autodiff do the rest.
  • Most implementations drop the $\gamma^t$. That is no longer the gradient of $J$ (Nota & Thomas, 2020), but it often works well.
  • Stochastic gradient ascent: needs decreasing $\alpha$, or mini-batches of episodes.

Let's practice: one REINFORCE update by hand

Tabular softmax, $\theta = 0$ (uniform), $\gamma = 0.9$, $\alpha = 1$. One episode:

$$s_1 \xrightarrow{\;\rightarrow\;} s_2 \xrightarrow{\;\uparrow\;} \text{small exit } (+1)$$

  1. Compute $G_0$ and $G_1$.
  2. Write the score $\nabla_{\theta_{s,\cdot}} \log \pi_\theta(a|s)$ at $\theta = 0$.
  3. Which entries of $\theta$ change, and by how much?
  4. New value of $\pi_\theta(\rightarrow|s_1)$?
Solution $G_1 = 1$, $G_0 = 0.9$. Score $= e_a - \tfrac14\mathbf{1}$. Weights $\gamma^0 G_0 = \gamma^1 G_1 = 0.9$, so $\theta_{s_1,\rightarrow} = \theta_{s_2,\uparrow} = 0.675$, and the 3 other entries of rows $s_1$, $s_2$ are $-0.225$. Then $\pi(\rightarrow|s_1) = \frac{e^{0.675}}{e^{0.675} + 3e^{-0.225}} \approx 0.45$.
Discussion: the episode went to the small exit, yet we increased π(→|s1), which is also the first move of the big-exit route. With r ≥ 0, every sampled action gets pushed up; only the relative sizes of the pushes carry information. This motivates baselines.
Part 5

Variance and baselines

Unbiased is not enough.

A policy with two parameters

Linear softmax with two features:

  • $\phi_{\text{big}}(s,a) = 1$ if $a$ is the shortest-route action to the big exit in $s$
  • $\phi_{\text{small}}(s,a) = 1$ if $a$ is the shortest-route action to the small exit in $s$
$$\pi_\theta(a|s) \propto \exp\big(\theta_{\text{big}}\, \phi_{\text{big}}(s,a) + \theta_{\text{small}}\, \phi_{\text{small}}(s,a)\big)$$
  • $\theta \in \R^2$: we can draw $J(\theta)$ and its gradient.
  • Two basins: a local optimum (small exit, $J \approx 0.9$) and the global one ($J \to 5\gamma^5 \approx 2.95$).
  • Already not concave with 2 parameters.

Why is $\hat g$ so noisy?

  • One episode = one sample of a high-dimensional random vector.
  • $G_t$ carries the randomness of all future actions and transitions.
  • $\hat g$ sums $T$ score terms: noise grows with the horizon.
  • All returns are $\ge 0$ here: every action taken is reinforced. The signal is only in the differences between pushes.
Two free lunches Both come from the same fact: the score has mean zero, $$\E_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s)\big] = \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0.$$ So we can remove terms whose expectation is zero, without introducing bias.

Trick 1: causality (reward-to-go)

The trajectory form multiplies every score by the full return $R(\tau)$. But an action cannot change the past:

Let $\mathcal{H}_t = (S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_t)$, the history before $A_t$. For $k \lt t$, $R_{k+1}$ is a function of $\mathcal{H}_t$, so

$$\E\big[R_{k+1}\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big] = \E\Big[R_{k+1}\, \underbrace{\E\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \,\big|\, \mathcal{H}_t\big]}_{=\,0}\Big] = 0$$

Drop those terms: $R(\tau) \to \gamma^t G_t$. This is how we got from the trajectory form to REINFORCE.

In our maze all the reward comes at the exit, so $\gamma^t G_t = R(\tau)$ for every $t$: here, causality gains nothing. It matters when rewards are spread along the way (the coins maze of Lecture 3).

Trick 2: baselines

For any function $b(s)$ that does not depend on the action:

$$\E\big[\, b(S_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \,\big|\, S_t \big] = b(S_t) \cdot 0 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{the estimator}$$
$$\hat g_b = \sum_t \gamma^t \big(G_t - \cfut{b(S_t)}\big)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t|S_t) \quad\text{is still unbiased.}$$

Common choices

  • constant: average return, $b = J(\theta)$ (in practice: a running mean)
  • state value: $b(s) = V^{\pi_\theta}(s)$. Then $G_t - V(S_t)$ estimates the advantage $A^\pi(S_t,A_t)$.
Intuition Push up an action only if it did better than expected from that state, push it down otherwise. The sign now carries information.
The baseline changes the variance, never the mean. V^π is not the variance-optimal baseline (see appendix) but it is close and easy to learn: with TD! That is actor–critic, next week.

Summary

  • Policy search: $\max_\theta J(\theta)$ by gradient ascent: one fixed objective, smooth in $\theta$.
  • $J(\pi) = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\langle d^\pi, r\rangle$: the occupancy measure is where a policy spends its time.
  • PG theorem: $\nabla J = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\E_{d^{\pi_\theta}}[Q^{\pi_\theta}\, \nabla \log \pi_\theta]$. No $\nabla d$, no $P$.
  • REINFORCE: replace $Q$ by the Monte Carlo return $G_t$. Unbiased, on-policy, simple.
  • Variance is the main issue. Causality and baselines reduce it for free.

Self-studyPG theorem: the unrolled proof (1/2)

Finite MDP, bounded rewards, $\pi_\theta$ differentiable. Start from $\Vpi(s) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s)\, \Qpi(s,a)$ and $\Qpi(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\Vpi(s')$:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla \Vpi(s) &= \sum_a \nabla\pi_\theta(a|s)\, \Qpi(s,a) + \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s)\, \nabla \Qpi(s,a) \\ &= \underbrace{\sum_a \nabla\pi_\theta(a|s)\, \Qpi(s,a)}_{=:\, \psi(s)} + \gamma \sum_{s'} P^\pi(s,s')\, \nabla \Vpi(s') \qquad (r \text{ does not depend on } \theta)\\ &= \psi(s) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P^\pi(s,s') \Big[\psi(s') + \gamma \sum_{s''} P^\pi(s',s'')\, \nabla \Vpi(s'')\Big] \\ &= \dots = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t \sum_{x} \P_\pi(S_t = x \mid S_0 = s)\; \psi(x). \end{aligned}$$

The remainder after $k$ unrollings is $\gamma^k (P^\pi)^k \nabla \Vpi$, bounded by $\gamma^k \max_s \|\nabla\Vpi(s)\| \to 0$.

Self-studyPG theorem: the unrolled proof (2/2)

Average over $S_0 \sim \rho$ and recognise the occupancy measure:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla J(\theta) &= \sum_x \underbrace{\sum_{t} \gamma^t \P_\pi(S_t = x)}_{=\, d^\pi(x)/(1-\gamma)}\; \sum_a \nabla\pi_\theta(a|x)\, \Qpi(x,a) = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{x,a} d^\pi(x)\, \pi_\theta(a|x)\, \Qpi(x,a)\, \nabla\log\pi_\theta(a|x). \end{aligned}$$
Equivalence with the trajectory forms Expanding $d^\pi$ back into a sum over time gives $\nabla J = \E_\tau\big[\sum_t \gamma^t \Qpi(S_t,A_t)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\big]$. Replacing $\Qpi(S_t,A_t)$ by $G_t$ is unbiased because $\E[G_t \mid S_0,A_0,\dots,S_t,A_t] = \Qpi(S_t,A_t)$ (Markov property). Finally, adding back the past rewards $\sum_{k \lt t}\gamma^k R_{k+1}$ (zero mean, see "causality") gives the full-return form $\E[R(\tau)\sum_t \nabla\log\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)]$.

Self-studyOccupancy measures and flow constraints

$d^\pi$ satisfies the constraints. Let $\mu_t(s) = \P_\pi(S_t = s)$. Then $\mu_0 = \rho$ and $\mu_{t+1}(s') = \sum_{s,a} P(s'|s,a)\,\pi(a|s)\,\mu_t(s)$. Multiply by $(1-\gamma)\gamma^{t+1}$ and sum over $t \ge 0$:

$$\textstyle d^\pi(s') - (1-\gamma)\rho(s') = \gamma \sum_{s,a} P(s'|s,a)\, d^\pi(s,a).$$

Conversely, let $d \ge 0$ satisfy the constraints and $\pi_d(a|s) = d(s,a)/\sum_b d(s,b)$. In vector form, with $\bar d(s) = \sum_a d(s,a)$, the constraints read $$\bar d^\top = (1-\gamma)\rho^\top + \gamma\, \bar d^\top P^{\pi_d},$$ whose unique solution is $\bar d^\top = (1-\gamma)\rho^\top (I - \gamma P^{\pi_d})^{-1}$, i.e. $\bar d = d^{\pi_d}$ on states. Then $d(s,a) = \bar d(s)\,\pi_d(a|s) = d^{\pi_d}(s,a)$.

States with $\bar d(s) = 0$ are never visited: define $\pi_d(\cdot|s)$ arbitrarily there.

One-step case: $g = (G - b)\, \nabla \log \pi_\theta(A|S)$ and a constant $b$ (per coordinate $i$). Since $\E[g]$ does not depend on $b$, minimise the second moment:
